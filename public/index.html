THE CONVERSATION

Who Gets to Make the Record?

Keenan Oliver on growing up in Mitchells Plain, making things with friends, and designing what remains

By Lubabalo Nkobo
Featuring Keenan Oliver

Keenan Oliver / Self-portrait / Office

ART / IN CONVERSATION / OCTOBER 2026

AN IMAGINED EXCHANGE WITH LUBABALO NKOBO, BASED ON KEENAN OLIVER’S ACCOUNT AND DOCUMENTED WORK.

Keenan Oliver began with a pen and pencil in Lentegeur, Mitchells Plain. Today he is the founder and creative director of Artefacts Office, working across identities, publications, exhibitions and spaces. Here, his former KAAS collaborator Lubabalo Nkobo presses him on what connects an early streetwear image to a listening bar, a book made after a library fire, and a museum exhibition. The answer keeps returning to who makes the record, and who gets to keep it.

Origins

LUBABALO NKOBO: You always bring the story back to drawing. Were you one of those kids who had a career plan at ten?

KEENAN OLIVER: No. I had a pen and pencil and I couldn't stop drawing. That's a very different thing from having a plan. I liked looking at something, then trying to work out how to make it appear on paper. It was a way of paying attention.

NKOBO: And you were doing that in Lentegeur?

OLIVER: That's where I'm from. Lentegeur, Mitchells Plain. I say that plainly because people often want an origin story to explain the work too neatly. A place is more complicated than that. It gives you people, habits, humour, ways of seeing. It also tells you something about who is allowed to move through Cape Town easily and who has to work at it.

NKOBO: Cape Town can make its divisions look picturesque.

OLIVER: Exactly. We grew up with the language of a new South Africa, but the city still carried the map of the old one. You can feel that in distance, access, in whose work gets seen as culture and whose gets treated as local colour. I don't want to make that the only lens for my childhood, but I can't pretend it wasn't there.

NKOBO: When did drawing become design?

OLIVER: In high school I took a design subject in graphics at Tygerberg Art Centre. Suddenly there was a discipline around something I had been doing instinctively. Composition, communication, making decisions for a reason. I could see that an image wasn't only something to look at. It could organise information and change how somebody understood it.

NKOBO: Then you took Marketing at CPUT. That's a turn.

OLIVER: It looks like one on paper. I was still interested in images, but the course made me think about what happens after an image leaves your desk. Who is it for? How does it circulate? What does it promise? I didn't leave drawing behind. I started asking more questions of it.

NKOBO: Were you teaching yourself the rest?

OLIVER: A lot of it, yes. Not alone, though. “Self-taught” can make it sound like nobody helped you. I learned by making work, looking closely, working with other people, and being honest about what I didn't know. The internet opened up references and tools, but it didn't teach you judgement. That came from doing things and seeing what held up.

NKOBO: You were learning while the image itself was changing.

OLIVER: It became easier to make and publish one. That was exciting. Someone in Cape Town could put something into circulation without waiting for an institution to grant permission. At the same time, an image could be seen for a day and disappear under the next thousand. Access grew; so did the speed of forgetting. I think a lot of my work sits inside that tension.

KAAS / Photography: Keenan Oliver / Styling: Lubabalo Nkobo & Kaylynne Damonze

KAAS and the early archive

NKOBO: Is that why Autodidacts & Associates feels important now?

OLIVER: Yes. It was a way to make work with people around us and give it a life beyond the moment. Clothes, photography, graphic design, publishing: we weren't treating those as separate departments. We were figuring out a language together.

NKOBO: You make it sound very orderly. I remember us trying things until they worked.

OLIVER: [Laughs.] That's probably a better description. The order comes later, when you look back. KAAS was collaborative. You styled the headwear editorial with Kaylynne Damonze; I photographed it. Mzonke Maloney and Thorne Oliver were part of that wider Autodidacts & Associates world. If the images have any force, it comes from those relationships, not from one person arriving with a complete vision.

NKOBO: And Mitchells Plain wasn't scenery for the shoot.

OLIVER: No. That's a distinction I care about. The place wasn't a texture we borrowed to make the images feel “real”. We were working from a place and a network we knew. That doesn't mean every picture has to announce a social argument. It means you're accountable to what and whom you're showing.

NKOBO: The Leaf Apparel photographs have that same concern with process.

OLIVER: Salik Harris had been making clothes for years. With that archive, I wanted people to see more than the finished garment. There was thought, labour and history before the photograph. I said at the time that documenting your own work gives it a life beyond the moment and gives you some power over your story. I still believe that.

NKOBO: Because if you leave the record to someone else—

OLIVER: They can make your history smaller. In South Africa there are whole communities whose culture has been dismissed because the official record was thin, selective or written from outside. Making your own images doesn't fix that on its own. It does mean there is evidence: we were here, making things, thinking about what they meant.

Babbi Deli & Bar / Brand architecture and visual direction: Artefacts Office c/o Keenan Oliver

Babbi / Stamp and exterior / Cape Town

Babbi

NKOBO: Then you went from making images to making a place. What was Babbi to you?

OLIVER: I was a co-owner and a founding partner, and I worked on its brand architecture and visual direction through Artefacts Office. But Babbi was never just a visual identity. It was a deli and listening bar on Bree Street. Food, records, conversation, live music, the way people moved between those things—that was the experience.

NKOBO: You can't art-direct a room into being welcoming.

OLIVER: You can make decisions that help, but then people decide what the room becomes. That's what I liked. A record would change the pace. Someone would come for lunch and stay because they heard something they wanted to ask about. Someone else would bring a friend. Design wasn't sitting in a brand guide; it was being tested by what happened there every day.

NKOBO: It also sat in that strange Cape Town gap between a place being known and being advertised everywhere.

OLIVER: Babbi travelled through people as much as through posts. I don't want to romanticise that—word of mouth isn't a business plan—but it tells you something about trust. People described the room to each other because they'd felt something there. You can't manufacture that with a logo.

NKOBO: Is there a part of you that misses it?

OLIVER: Of course. It's closed now. A place can be intensely present and then suddenly it's gone. You still find pieces of it in photographs, posters, a stamp, somebody remembering a night. That made me think about how design records a social life without pretending to own it.

NKOBO: So Babbi belongs in the portfolio, but not as a tidy case study.

OLIVER: Right. If you only show the logo, you've left out the point. We were building the conditions for people to spend time together. The identity mattered because it belonged to that larger thing.

Lost Libraries, Burnt Archives / Book design and layout: Artefacts Office c/o Keenan Oliver / Cover artwork: Lady Skollie

Lost Libraries, Burnt Archives

NKOBO: Let's move from a place that closed to a library that burned. That sounds abrupt, but I know you see a connection.

OLIVER: The scale and the stakes are different. Lost Libraries, Burnt Archives responded to the 2021 fire at UCT's Jagger Library and the loss of collections there. Sindi-Leigh McBride and Julia Rensing edited the publication. It brings together writing and art from 22 contributors. My work was the book design and layout through Artefacts Office.

NKOBO: What do you do as a designer when the subject is loss?

OLIVER: First, you listen to the material. I didn't write those texts, curate the collection or make the cover artwork. Lady Skollie made the cover artwork. My job was to give the different contributions a coherent reading structure without making them feel as if they all spoke in one voice.

NKOBO: That's harder than making everything match.

OLIVER: Much harder. An archive carries disagreement, unevenness, gaps. The design needs enough order for someone to move through it, but not so much that it tidies away what happened. The book was also a physical object made in a small edition for libraries and archives, with a freely available digital edition. How it reached people was part of its meaning.

NKOBO: You've used the word “record” since the streetwear work. Does it feel different here?

OLIVER: Absolutely. You have to be careful not to flatten everything into one personal theme. A devastating fire isn't a metaphor for a closed bar. But working on that publication sharpened a question I already had: who decides what is kept, and what do we do when something can't be recovered? Design can't restore a destroyed collection. It can help make space for many responses to be read and remembered.

We Proceed in the Footsteps of the Sunlight / Identity: Artefacts Office / Exhibition photography: Slater Studio & Dillon Marsh

Zohra Opoku and Artefacts Office

NKOBO: Where does Zohra Opoku's exhibition sit in this story?

OLIVER: In a different place again. We Proceed in the Footsteps of the Sunlight at Zeitz MOCAA brought Zohra's work into a museum survey. Her own visual and material language is already so considered: photography, fabric, stitching, screen printing, the movement between image and object. Artefacts Office worked on the exhibition identity, and I designed the monograph.

NKOBO: You couldn't arrive with a design signature and put it over the work.

OLIVER: Nor would I want to. The task was to make a framework that could meet the work with attention. An exhibition identity has to operate in a museum, in public communication and around the work itself. The book has another kind of life. It has to hold the images and writing in sequence, at a pace a reader can choose, long after they've left the exhibition.

NKOBO: That's one of the things people miss about books. They think the design is the cover.

OLIVER: The cover matters. But then you open it. What appears first? How large is an image? When do you have to turn the page? Where does a text begin to breathe? Those decisions shape an encounter with the work. They're quiet, but they aren't neutral.

NKOBO: You founded Artefacts Office. Did you know you wanted it to move between a bar, a book and a museum?

OLIVER: I knew I didn't want to organise my work by medium alone. As founder and creative director, I can build a practice around the question a project is asking, then decide what form it needs. Sometimes that's an identity system. Sometimes it's a publication, exhibition graphics or a space. The scale changes; the responsibility to the subject and the people involved doesn't.

NKOBO: You also work with brands. Does this all become too serious when you explain it that way?

OLIVER: [Laughs.] Maybe. I work with brands, artists and institutions, and each comes with a different purpose. A commercial brief still needs intelligence and specificity. I don't think every project has to carry the weight of an archive. I do think the work should know where it is, whom it's addressing and what it's putting into the world.

NKOBO: You started by drawing what you saw. Is that still in there?

OLIVER: Yes. Drawing made me look closely. The tools have changed and the work has become more collaborative, but that part hasn't. I still want to understand what's in front of me before I decide what to make of it.

NKOBO: And after you make it?

OLIVER: I want it to have a life with other people. Then I want there to be a record of how it got there.

Keenan Oliver is the founder and creative director of Artefacts Office. This is a newly written, constructed editorial conversation, not a transcript of a recorded interview.
