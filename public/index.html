A Conversation

Making Records

October 2026Lubabalo Nkobo

Long-time friends and collaborators Lubabalo Nkobo and Keenan Oliver trace a practice built through drawing, publishing, place and the making of records — from KAAS and Babbi to Lost Libraries, Burnt Archives and Zohra Opoku.

Keenan Oliver / Self-portrait / Office
IMAGE — PERSONAL ARCHIVE / EARLY DRAWINGS
IMAGE — EARLY CAPE TOWN / PEOPLE / OBJECT

“I picked up a pen and pencil when I was young and never really put them down.”

LUBABALO NKOBO: You’ve told me before that drawing came first. What do you remember about that?

KEENAN OLIVER: I don't remember deciding that I wanted to become a designer. I remember drawing. I liked making things with a pen and pencil and trying to make the things I was seeing exist again on paper.

Then in high school I started taking graphics more seriously through my design subject and my time at Tygerberg Art Centre. That was probably the first time I understood that the things I was instinctively doing had a language and a discipline around them.

LN: And then CPUT?

KO: I studied Marketing. Strategy, consumer behaviour, brand communication, research. It sounds like a different direction, but I think it was useful because I've always been interested in what happens when an image enters the world. How it communicates, who sees it, what it asks from people.

LN: When I think about the early work, it feels less like “a career beginning” and more like a period where you were just trying things.

KO: That's probably accurate. I moved through photography, publications, music videos, social media, commercial film. Those things were foundational. I'm less interested in presenting them as a résumé than in what they taught me about looking, sequencing, production and making things happen with whatever was available.

The image changed while I was learning how to make it.

LN: There are a few early projects that still matter when we talk about where the practice came from.

KO: Yeah. Autodidacts & Associates, KAAS, the self-publishing work. They were important because that was when photography, clothes, design, place and people started to exist together for me.

LN: And you and I were actually working together on KAAS. You were photographing, I was styling.

KO: Exactly. That's part of why I don't like thinking about those projects only as “my work.” They came out of a group of people making something together. The Bubblegum Club record credits you for styling the KAAS shoot, with Keenan Oliver on photography, art direction and design alongside Mzonke Maloney, and Thorne Oliver part of the wider Autodidacts & Associates group. That collaborative structure matters to the story.

And Mitchells Plain mattered because it wasn't somewhere we were importing a story into. We were making something from inside a place and a network we actually knew.

LN: What did that period teach you that stayed?

KO: That making an image can also be making a record. And that the people inside the image should have some relationship to the story around it.

LN: Did that make documentation important to you?

KO: I think so. The projects made me conscious of what disappears when nobody documents it. You can make something meaningful and six months later there is no trace of how it came together, who was there, what the place felt like.

That's why those early projects matter to me, but they're not the destination. They're the foundation underneath the work that came after.

The practice became a place

Babbi

LN: Babbi feels like the first time the practice really left the page.

KO: Yeah. Because the work was happening in real time, with people, every day. I was a founding partner responsible for the brand architecture and visual direction, but the identity was only one layer of it. We were making a place.

It was small, tucked away off Bree Street, down an alley. You could come for food or stay for the music. You could sit outside, hear a record, talk, meet somebody. The room was doing a lot before you even started talking about “design.”

LN: And it wasn't trying to behave like a typical venue.

KO: That's part of what made it interesting. The Mail & Guardian wrote about it as a small listening bar with very little social media presence, relying largely on word of mouth. It was apparently packed most nights and had expanded from food, wine and records into live music.

That “need-to-know” quality mattered. You weren't being sold an identity before you arrived. The place itself was the discovery.

LN: The music seems central to what Babbi became.

KO: Completely. The records weren't decoration. They were part of the architecture. Somebody was choosing what you heard. That changes the behaviour in a room.

And the audience wasn't one type. Musicians, designers, artists, people working in offices, visitors, people who simply wanted a good night. The value was in the mix.

LN: That seems like a cultural function, not just a hospitality function.

KO: I think it became that. People trust a room because of how it makes them feel, then they start returning. Once people return, the place becomes a network. Conversations happen. Collaborations happen. Someone meets someone else. A record gets played that changes somebody's night.

The wider life of the room

Babbi became part of a Cape Town ecology where music, food, fashion, design and social life could occupy the same small space.

LN: The outside record of Babbi backs that up.

KO: Yeah. Condé Nast Traveller picked it out in a guide to Cape Town's food and culture, describing the venue beneath Pot Plant Club as a hidden underground space with a courtyard, vinyl collection and performance area that could move from lunch to a gig in the evening.

The same kind of cultural overlap appears in GIDA Journal Volume III's Cape Town programme, which used the Pot Plant Club / Babbi ecosystem through music, food and a late-night gathering.

Then in 2025, Design as an Optimistic Act was hosted at 215 Bree Street by the team behind Pot Plant Club and Babbi. The exhibition was explicitly about intersections of design, African cities and future-making. So the room kept becoming a place where different cultural practices could cross.

LN: What did Babbi teach you about design?

KO: That people aren't an audience you arrive at after the design is finished. They're part of the material.

You can design a beautiful identity, but if the place feels cold, you've missed it. You can have good music, but if nobody wants to stay, you've missed something. You can have good food, but if there is no reason to talk, you've missed something. The whole thing is the design.

LN: So sound became part of the system.

KO: Sound is a system. Behaviour is a system. Hospitality is a system. Memory is a system. Babbi taught me to think about those things together.

LN: And now that it's closed?

KO: It makes the record more important. Corner now records Babbi as permanently closed, but it still describes the little vinyl bar in terms of its records, objects, atmosphere and the choice between conversation upstairs and the music downstairs.

Some places only exist for a short time, but while they're there they change who meets who. They create references for the next thing.

Babbi was a room, but it was also a piece of Cape Town's cultural memory.

LN: Lost Libraries, Burnt Archives feels like a major turning point. Why?

KO: Because the subject and the function of the book are almost the same thing. The project responds to the 2021 fire at Jagger Library at UCT, where the African Studies Special Collection and other important collections were destroyed. The publication came out of a collaborative project around the memorial exhibition and the salvage work that followed.

It's a collection of short stories, artworks, poems and essays by 22 contributors. So you're designing a single object that has to contain very different voices and forms, all circling ideas of archives, libraries, collective loss, commemoration and knowledge.

LN: Did that change how you thought about the book as an object?

KO: Absolutely. A library is an organised record of things people have decided are worth keeping. Then something happens and that record can disappear. The book is responding to that loss by making another record.

It also wasn't a normal commercial book. The physical edition was limited to 100 copies, handmade by Pulp Paperworks, and made available to university and public libraries, research institutions and specialist archives in South Africa and selected international institutions. The ebook was made freely available.

So the publication itself becomes part of the archive it is thinking about.

LN: That sounds very close to what you've been talking about since the early work — making sure something remains.

KO: Exactly. But here the stakes are much bigger. It isn't just a personal record. You're dealing with cultural memory, institutional memory, knowledge that was lost, and the effort to reconstruct some form of continuity.

LN: And how did you handle all those contributors?

KO: You need enough structure for the reader to move through the book, but not so much structure that everything starts sounding like one voice. I like that kind of design problem. A strong editorial system doesn't erase difference. It gives difference somewhere to live.

LN: Then there is Zohra Opoku at Zeitz MOCAA. This feels like another level of the practice.

KO: It was a major project for me. The exhibition brings together a decade of Zohra's work and it moves through photography, screen printing, sewing, sculpture and installation. Her personal history, cultural inheritance, memory and identity are embedded in the materials as much as in the subject matter.

So the design has to be attentive. You're not imposing a visual identity on a blank canvas. You're working with an artist who already has a very developed language.

LN: The exhibition is organised around Ground, Breath and Water.

KO: Yes. Those are more than categories. They give you a way of moving through the work. Ground becomes belonging, rootedness, family and identity. Breath connects to spirit and the space between life and death. Water carries fluidity, ritual and direction.

I liked that the exhibition isn't simply saying “here is ten years of work.” It creates a different kind of movement through the practice.

LN: And the monograph extends that movement.

KO: Exactly. The monograph is 198 pages and brings together critical reflections, curatorial writing and an interview with Zohra alongside the work. Zeitz MOCAA credits me as the publication designer.

What was interesting was making a publication that could be related to the exhibition without just reproducing it. The book has to have its own rhythm. It has to work when the museum isn't around it.

LN: What did the project teach you?

KO: Material memory. A photograph can be screen-printed onto fabric, then stitched, cut, collaged, transformed. The image keeps changing form. That made me think about the book in a similar way — the object isn't just carrying information about the work. Sequence, scale, paper, pacing, the relationship between images — those things become part of the meaning.

LN: The title itself is about movement.

KO: Yes. It comes from the Book of the Dead, and in the context of the exhibition it becomes a declaration about moving through turbulence and continuing toward what comes next.

That felt relevant to the whole project. A survey can look backward, but it can also be about what is being carried forward. What remains, what changes, what keeps becoming.

The work keeps changing scale.
The question stays the same.

LN: Is that the thread between Babbi, Lost Libraries and Zohra?

KO: I think so. They're very different projects, but each one is concerned with what remains.

Babbi was a place. It existed through people, sound, food, objects and memory. Lost Libraries is literally thinking about an archive after destruction. Zohra's work is about memory, ancestry, identity and material — and the monograph becomes another object through which that work can keep travelling.

They're all different kinds of records.

LN: How important is having your own perspective to the work?

KO: It’s essential. I think the perspective is the part you bring that nobody else can. Skills can be shared, references can be shared, even ways of working can be learned — but the way you see something comes from your own experience, your own memory, your own taste, the people you've spent time with and the places that have shaped you.

For me, that perspective isn't about trying to make everything autobiographical. It's more about being honest about where I'm looking from. I don't want to make work that could have been made by anyone. Even when I'm working within a brief, there has to be some point where my way of seeing enters the work.

I think that's also why the projects that matter most to me have some sense of specificity. They feel connected to a person, a place, a group of people or a particular moment. The perspective is what gives the work its reason to exist beyond just being well made.

LN: And now?

KO: I'm still interested in how culture gets made and remembered while everything around it gets faster. Images are easier to produce, easier to circulate and easier to forget. The technology changes, but the question of memory doesn't.

Artefacts Office is the structure that lets me keep working across identity, publishing, exhibition, image-making and space without pretending those things are unrelated.

LN: So what's the simplest way you would describe what you're doing?

I'm still trying to look closely, make something from what I find, and keep a record of the fact that it happened.
